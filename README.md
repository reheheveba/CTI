## Attack lifecycle
### 1. Reconnaissance

The operator identifies target companies, employees, help-desk procedures, Salesforce usage, public SSO portals, contractors, and potentially exposed secrets in public code repositories. Public-facing employee data is useful for convincing social-engineering calls and for impersonating IT staff.

**Defensive implication:** Reduce publicly exposed identity and technical details where practical. Continuously scan public repositories for credentials, tokens, API keys, connection strings, and OAuth secrets.

### 2. Initial Access Through Vishing

UNC6040 activity associated with the Salesforce campaign used voice phishing to impersonate IT support and deceive users into granting access to Salesforce.

Common attacker objectives during such calls include:

- Obtaining a password and MFA code through a fake SSO page
- Convincing the target to approve an MFA push notification
- Directing the target to authorize an OAuth application
- Manipulating help-desk staff into resetting passwords or MFA factors
- Getting the victim to run a tool or connect an application that appears operationally legitimate

**Detection hypothesis:** A help-desk interaction, MFA reset, or new application authorization immediately followed by an unfamiliar SaaS login is a high-confidence incident precursor.

### 3. Identity Compromise and OAuth Abuse

After social engineering succeeds, the attacker may operate through a legitimate account, authenticated browser session, application access token, or malicious/unauthorized OAuth consent. This makes traditional endpoint-only detection insufficient.

- Registration of a new MFA authenticator, passkey, phone number, recovery email, or security key
- Password reset or forced reauthentication followed by a new-device login
- OAuth consent granted to an unfamiliar enterprise application
- Granting high-risk permissions such as offline access, read/write mail, file access, CRM access, or directory access
- Creation or modification of service principals, API integrations, or long-lived tokens

### 4. SaaS Collection

ShinyHunters has collected Salesforce datasets from victims in airline and retail sectors. The group and associated clusters can use normal CRM interfaces, reports, exports, bulk APIs, and Data Loader-style functionality to collect customer and business data.

Potential collection sources include:

- Salesforce objects, reports, contacts, cases, leads, opportunities, and account records
- SharePoint or OneDrive document repositories
- Microsoft 365 mailboxes and collaboration content
- Cloud storage, ticketing systems, knowledge bases, and business applications integrated with SSO

### 5. Data Exfiltration

ShinyHunters has used compromised Salesforce CRM dashboards to exfiltrate bulk data. This is operationally important because much of the traffic can be legitimate HTTPS communication to an approved SaaS provider.

**High-risk indicators:**

- Bulk report exports
- Salesforce Data Loader or Bulk API activity inconsistent with a user’s role
- Unusual API volume or sequential access across large record sets
- Mass downloads from SharePoint, OneDrive, Google Drive, or CRM attachments
- Export activity from a newly seen device, IP address, ASN, geography, or OAuth client
- Collection occurring overnight, on weekends, or immediately after identity changes

### 6. Extortion and Impact

In the Salesforce campaign, victims received extortion messages from UNC6240, which claimed the ShinyHunters identity. Reports documented Bitcoin payment demands with a 72-hour deadline.

The FBI warns that threat actors may use real or exaggerated claims of access to sensitive information to pressure victims and may make claims about material that does not actually exist. Incident responders should therefore verify any extortion claim through forensic evidence rather than assuming all claimed data was stolen.

## Detection and Hunting

### Required Telemetry

Prioritize complete, retained, and centrally searchable logs from:

- Identity provider: Entra ID, Okta, Ping, Google Workspace, or equivalent
- MFA and password-reset systems
- OAuth consent, application registration, service principal, and token events
- Salesforce Event Monitoring, Login History, Setup Audit Trail, API, Bulk API, report-export, and Data Loader logs
- Microsoft 365 Unified Audit Log, SharePoint, OneDrive, Exchange, and Defender for Cloud Apps
- CASB, DLP, secure web gateway, proxy, VPN, DNS, EDR, and SOAR
- Help-desk ticketing, call records, and identity-verification records

### Priority Detections

| Detection | Logic | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| MFA change followed by SaaS export | New MFA method, password reset, recovery-method change, or device registration followed by data export within 24 hours | Critical |
| New OAuth consent followed by bulk access | New or rare OAuth app grant followed by abnormal Salesforce, SharePoint, OneDrive, or mailbox activity | Critical |
| Suspicious Salesforce bulk extraction | New IP/device/ASN plus Bulk API, Data Loader, large report export, or abnormal API volume | High |
| Identity compromise sequence | Help-desk reset or unusual sign-in followed by new MFA enrollment, token activity, or SaaS access | Critical |
| Impossible travel / unfamiliar login | Successful authentication from unusual geography, ASN, TOR/VPN, device, browser, or user-agent | High |
| Privileged SaaS export | Large CRM/file export by a privileged user or outside approved operational windows | High |
| Extortion sender indicator | Inbound/outbound investigation related to `shinycorp@tuta[.]com` or `shinygroup@tuta[.]com`; treat as historic campaign-specific indicators, not universal attribution | Medium |

### Correlation Rule

Raise a **critical data-extortion incident** if a single identity produces two or more of the following signals within 24 hours:

```text
1. Password reset, MFA-factor addition/removal, recovery-data change,
   passkey registration, or help-desk identity-verification event.

2. New device, unusual user-agent, new IP, new country, unusual ASN,
   TOR/VPN egress, or impossible-travel sign-in.

3. New OAuth consent, high-privilege OAuth scope, suspicious service
   principal, application registration, or token issuance.

4. Salesforce Bulk API/Data Loader/report export or high-volume CRM queries.

5. Large SharePoint, OneDrive, cloud-storage, or mailbox export/download.

6. Access outside the user’s normal working hours or outside their normal
   data-access baseline.
```

### Immediate Containment Actions

1. Revoke active sessions and refresh tokens for the affected identity.
2. Disable or reset the suspected account after preserving evidence.
3. Remove or disable suspicious OAuth grants, service principals, integrations, and MFA methods.
4. Block bulk exports where operationally feasible.
5. Preserve identity, SaaS, proxy, CASB, and help-desk logs.
6. Scope data access by object, file, mailbox, CRM report, API request, and export job.
7. Activate legal, privacy, communications, executive leadership, and incident-response procedures.

### Hunting Query Logic

```text
Find users who, within 24 hours:

- Changed or enrolled an MFA method
- Reset a password or recovery factor
- Granted consent to an OAuth application
- Authenticated from a new device, IP, ASN, or unusual geography

AND then:

- Exported Salesforce reports
- Used Salesforce Bulk API or Data Loader
- Downloaded unusually high volumes of CRM, SharePoint, or OneDrive data
- Queried large record sets beyond their historical baseline
```

Use behavioral baselines rather than relying only on fixed download thresholds. A sales-operations administrator may legitimately export thousands of records; the same action by an HR user, a new device, or a recently reset account should be treated very differently.

## Indicators and IOC Handling
```
| Indicator Type| Value                  |Role                     | Confidence                                  | Handling |
| Email address | `shinycorp@tuta[.]com` | Extortion communication | High for the cited campaign; time-sensitive | Search mail telemetry, preserve evidence, block according to policy

```
IOC limitations matter. Email addresses, phishing domains, IP addresses, and hosting infrastructure can change quickly. Do not rely on historic indicators as the primary defense. Behavioral indicators—vishing, abnormal identity changes, OAuth abuse, and abnormal SaaS export activity—are more durable and more valuable for detection.

## Defensive Priorities

1. **Deploy phishing-resistant MFA.** Require FIDO2 security keys or passkeys for administrators, help desk, identity administrators, Salesforce admins, and users who can perform bulk exports. Push-based MFA alone is vulnerable to convincing social engineering and MFA fatigue.

2. **Harden help-desk verification.** Do not reset passwords, MFA methods, recovery details, or privileged access solely based on a phone call. Require independent identity verification and out-of-band confirmation. Treat urgent requests to change MFA as possible vishing.

3. **Restrict OAuth consent and application access.** Block end-user consent to unverified applications; require administrator approval for privileged scopes; maintain an allowlist of trusted publishers; review existing enterprise apps, service principals, API integrations, and long-lived tokens.

4. **Limit SaaS export capability.** Apply least privilege to Salesforce export functions, Bulk API access, Data Loader, reporting, CRM administration, and cloud-file downloads. Use approval workflows or just-in-time access for high-impact functions.

5. **Monitor cloud-scale exfiltration.** Baseline normal API activity and exports; alert on deviations by volume, destination, time, device, user role, geography, OAuth client, and API method.

6. **Preserve sufficient log retention.** Ensure identity, OAuth, Salesforce, Microsoft 365, SharePoint, OneDrive, CASB, DLP, proxy, DNS, and help-desk logs are retained long enough for retrospective scoping and regulatory investigation.

7. **Run vishing exercises.** Employees and help-desk teams should practice terminating unsolicited “IT support” calls, independently calling known internal numbers, and refusing to reveal passwords, MFA codes, recovery codes, or approval prompts.

## Assessment

### High Confidence

- ShinyHunters is a financially motivated data-theft and extortion actor tracked as G1057 and associated with UNC6240/Bling Libra.
- The actor or its associated extortion activity targets valuable PII and business data for resale or extortion.
- The October 2024 Salesforce campaign involved vishing-based initial access by UNC6040 and extortion by UNC6240 claiming the ShinyHunters name.
- Salesforce data collection and web-service exfiltration are documented behaviors.
- Defending identity, OAuth, help desk, and SaaS exports is more important than relying solely on endpoint malware detection.

### Medium Confidence

- OAuth/application-token abuse is a key enabling mechanism in some related campaigns and should be a primary defensive hypothesis.
- Related actor ecosystems may collaborate, overlap, or reuse branding, but exact operator-level relationships should not be asserted without campaign-specific evidence.

### Operational Conclusion

Treat a sequence of **vishing or help-desk interaction → identity/MFA or OAuth change → unfamiliar SaaS login → bulk CRM/cloud export → extortion contact** as a potential high-severity data breach.

Initiate containment and scoping even when endpoint telemetry shows no malware or ransomware execution.
